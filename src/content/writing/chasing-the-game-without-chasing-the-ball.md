Pressing higher is only one way of increasing risk. A trailing team needs to increase its probability of scoring, not necessarily its defensive line of engagement.

![A tactical illustration of a trailing team changing its approach](/mustafabarak/images/writing/trailing-team-tactic.png)

You are 1–0 down with 25 minutes left. The conventional reaction is predictable: push the defensive line higher, press the opponent's centre-backs, recover the ball closer to their goal and increase the pressure. But what if your team is simply not built for that? Perhaps your centre-backs struggle defending large spaces. Perhaps your forwards are intelligent with the ball but ineffective pressers. Perhaps the team lacks the physical capacity to sustain an aggressive press. In that case, asking the team to press high because the score is 0–1 may actually make the opponent's job easier.

Being behind requires taking more risk. It does not necessarily require pressing higher.

## Compact mid-block

One alternative is to keep a compact mid-block but become much more selective about **where** you want to recover the ball. Instead of pressing the centre-backs continuously, allow them to circulate possession. Close the central options and encourage the pass toward a full-back or another predetermined player. That pass becomes the trigger.

You are not refusing to press. You are choosing when pressing is worth the risk.

## Invitation

There is another interesting possibility: **invite the opponent forward.**

When protecting a lead, many teams naturally become more conservative. If you immediately push ten metres higher, you may allow them to sit deep, play directly behind your press and attack the space you have created.

Instead, allowing their defenders and midfielders slightly more territory can sometimes create the space that your attack needs. Recovering the ball around midfield against an opponent whose full-backs and midfielders have moved forward can produce a much better attacking situation than recovering it near their penalty area against nine players already behind the ball.

The aggression can therefore come **after the recovery rather than before it**.

Once possession is won, the behaviour changes. The first thought becomes vertical. Wingers immediately attack depth. A midfielder runs beyond the striker. The first or second pass looks forward rather than automatically recycling possession. You can dramatically increase your attacking risk without dramatically changing your defensive structure.

The same principle applies when you have established possession. Full-backs can move higher. Another midfielder can occupy the box. Centre-backs can carry the ball further forward. More players can attack crosses and second balls.

This is where an important distinction appears:

**Defensive risk and attacking risk do not have to increase together.**

A team can defend in almost the same 4-4-2 mid-block at 0–0 and 0–1, yet behave completely differently once it wins the ball.

Of course, as the clock approaches 90 minutes, the acceptable level of risk changes. Eventually there may be little value in protecting spaces when defeat is almost certain anyway. But with 20 or 30 minutes remaining, going behind should not automatically destroy the tactical structure chosen for your players.

The objective is not to move the press higher. The objective is to create more and better opportunities to score. Sometimes the best way to chase the game is to stop chasing the ball.
