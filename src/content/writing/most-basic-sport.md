There is something strange about football that I don’t see to the same extent in many other sports.

You can watch football for the first time and understand what is happening almost immediately.

There are two goals. Two teams are trying to put the ball into the other team’s goal. You cannot use your hands. There is an offside rule that might take a little longer to understand, but even without fully understanding it, you can watch a match and enjoy it.

More importantly, you can start having opinions almost immediately.

*They should attack from the left.*

*Why did he take that player off?*

*They need another striker.*

*The midfield is terrible.*

*Why are they passing backwards?*

You don’t need to understand build-up structures, pressing triggers, rest defence or positional play to participate in a football conversation. And this is probably one of football’s greatest strengths.

But it also creates a very interesting situation.

![Two people watching the same game with different depths of football knowledge](/mustafabarak/images/writing/they_should_attack.png)

## We are watching the same match, but not necessarily seeing the same game

The deeper you go into football, the stranger it becomes.

A formation saying 4-3-3 suddenly tells you very little.

What does the team become in possession? Is it a 3-2-5? Does one full-back invert? Who provides the width? How are they trying to create a free man during the first phase of build-up?

Then there is the opponent.

How are they pressing that structure? Are they intentionally allowing one centre-back to receive the ball? Are they using cover shadows to block the defensive midfielder? What triggers their press? What happens if the first line is broken?

And what happens after possession is lost?

Suddenly, what looked like 22 people trying to move a ball towards a goal becomes a constantly changing system of space, numerical advantages, positioning, risk and decision-making.

This creates an unusual gap between people talking about football.

Imagine two people watching the same match.

One says:

“The midfield was terrible today. They couldn’t keep the ball.”

The other might say:

“The opponent blocked the central progression by pressing the centre-backs while using cover shadows on the number six. The team never found a good solution in the first phase, so they were repeatedly forced into longer passes.”

The first person may not even be wrong.

That is important.

Both people have observed the same problem. But one is describing **what happened**, while the other is trying to understand **the mechanism that produced it**.

That difference can be enormous.

## Football has an almost zero entry barrier and an incredibly high ceiling

I think this is one of the reasons football became such a powerful global sport.

It doesn’t require expertise before it rewards you.

You can be eight years old and have an opinion about a substitution. You can watch five matches in your life and argue that a team should play with two strikers. You can sit in a café with friends and spend two hours discussing what the manager should have done.

Football allows everyone into the conversation.

And I don’t think we should look down on that.

In fact, I think that accessibility is part of what makes serious football analysis possible in the first place.

The enormous audience creates enormous commercial value. That commercial value creates professional clubs with enormous budgets. Those budgets create investment in academies, coaching, scouting, performance science, data infrastructure and technology.

Today clubs can work with event data, tracking data, expected-goals models, opposition analysis departments, dedicated set-piece coaches, video analysts, data scientists and sophisticated scouting systems.

There is something beautifully circular about this.

Millions of people casually watching football create the economic ecosystem that allows a much smaller group of people to study football at an extraordinary level of detail.

In other words, the guy shouting “just shoot!” at his television is, indirectly, part of the reason someone else can build a model analysing thousands of possessions.

## The problem is not having simple opinions

I don’t think there is anything wrong with watching football casually.

Football would probably be a worse sport if everyone needed to understand pressing structures before being allowed to have an opinion.

The interesting distinction is not between people who “know football” and people who “don’t know football.”

It is whether we understand **the level at which we are discussing it**.

There is a progression that I find useful:

**What happened?**

**Why did it happen?**

**Was it intentional?**

**How did the opponent respond?**

**What could have been done differently?**

And finally:

**What would that alternative have cost?**

That last question might be the most important one.

“Push the full-backs higher.”

Fine. Who protects the space when possession is lost?

“Press higher.”

With which players? Against which build-up structure? What happens if the opponent breaks the first line?

“Play another striker.”

Fine. Which player are you removing, and what happens to your numerical situation in midfield?

Football becomes much more interesting when you stop treating tactical decisions as isolated solutions.

Every solution creates another problem.

Every advantage requires giving something up somewhere else.

And perhaps understanding football is not really about knowing what a team *should* do.

It is about becoming better at seeing the trade-offs behind what they choose to do.

## Maybe that contradiction is football’s greatest strength

Football is often described as a simple game.

It is.

And it absolutely isn’t.

That contradiction may be one of the best things about it.

Its entry barrier is almost zero, while its intellectual ceiling seems almost unlimited.

Someone can watch a match and simply enjoy a winger dribbling past three defenders. Someone else can watch the same sequence and think about how the build-up manipulated the defensive block to create the one-versus-one in the first place.

Both can love football.

Both can talk about football.

They may even believe they are talking about exactly the same thing.

But sometimes, they are watching two completely different games.

And somehow, both games fit inside the same 90 minutes.
