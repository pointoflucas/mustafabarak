There is something almost uncomfortable about *The Myth of Sisyphus*.

Camus begins with a brutal question: if life has no ultimate meaning, why continue living it?

His answer is not particularly comforting. He does not give us God, destiny, justice, or some hidden purpose waiting to be discovered. Instead, he asks us to accept the absurd: we want life to make sense, but the universe does not owe us an explanation.

Sisyphus becomes the perfect image of this. He pushes a rock up a mountain, watches it roll back down, and then walks after it to begin again. Forever.

![Not a single man](/mustafabarak/images/writing/esfj-take.png)

Camus famously asks us to imagine Sisyphus happy.

I understand the idea, but I think there is another way to look at his mountain.

Most of our lives are already full of small versions of that rock. We wake up, work, solve problems, clean the house, worry about people, celebrate something, lose something, and eventually repeat most of it again. Even the things we achieve rarely stay “finished.” There is always another problem, another responsibility, another Monday.

From a purely cosmic perspective, perhaps none of this matters very much.

But human beings rarely experience life from a cosmic perspective.

A meal prepared for someone matters to the person eating it. Showing up when a friend is having a terrible week matters to that friend. Remembering someone's birthday, helping your family, doing your job properly because others depend on it — none of these things solve the philosophical problem of existence.

Yet they make existence worth participating in.

This is where I find myself slightly distant from Camus. He places enormous weight on the individual's confrontation with the absurd. There is something powerful in that independence: I know the rock will fall, and I push anyway.

But perhaps meaning does not have to exist inside the rock or at the top of the mountain.

Perhaps it exists around it.

Maybe someone is walking beside Sisyphus. Maybe someone is waiting for him when he comes down. Maybe he pushes a little harder one day because someone needs him to.

None of this defeats the absurd. The rock still falls.

But it changes what pushing it means.

I don't think we need to prove that life has some grand objective meaning before allowing ordinary things to matter. Meaning can be much smaller than that. Temporary, local, even fragile. Two people can create something meaningful between them even if the universe remains completely indifferent to both.

And perhaps that is enough.

Camus taught me not to wait for the universe to explain itself.

But if I have to push the rock anyway, I would rather have people around the mountain.
